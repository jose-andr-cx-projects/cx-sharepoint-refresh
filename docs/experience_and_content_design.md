# Experience and Content Design

Status: Draft  
Current phase: Define and Develop (23 September–13 October 2026)  
Last updated: 30 September 2026

## Purpose

Define the future CX employee experience as discovery matures.

## Design intent

The future experience should help employees:

1. understand what CX can help them with;
2. find relevant tools, services, information or guidance;
3. identify trusted and authoritative information;
4. know the next step;
5. reach the right source, service or specialist content.

## Working experience model

The emerging model is:

**Employee need → CoMWeb guidance → authoritative source or service → clear next step**

CoMWeb should guide, orient and connect employees rather than hold everything itself.

## Experience principles

### Employee needs first

Organise around what employees are trying to understand, find or do rather than around branch ownership.

### One authoritative source

Provide enough context to help employees understand what they need, then connect them to the right source instead of duplicating content.

### Multiple ways in

Support navigation, search, CoMpanion and direct links.

### Lead with the CX offer

Make it clear what employees can come to CX for and what they can do next.

### Design for trust

Make currency, ownership and authoritative source clear.

### Use the minimum useful experience

Do not create new pages by default. Prefer updating, consolidating, linking or surfacing before creating net-new content.

## Emerging employee-need frame

A current CoMWeb recommendation organises content around broad employee needs rather than branch structure.

For CX and related support, the emerging employee need is:

> How do I understand, engage and respond to customers and communities?

This is a working direction and should be treated as current guidance rather than a finalised information architecture.

## Resource hub direction

The current CoMWeb model suggests specialist needs can sit within resource hubs.

For CX, an emerging `Customers and Communities` hub could orient employees to needs such as:

- understand customer and community insights;
- engage customers and communities;
- improve customer communication and experiences;
- build capability;
- manage customer feedback and complaints.

The final scope and structure require validation.

## Information architecture criteria

Each category should:

- represent a meaningful employee need;
- use understandable employee language;
- avoid duplicating authoritative content;
- be distinguishable from other categories;
- support multiple discovery pathways;
- have clear ownership;
- be maintainable over time.

## Content-home model

### CoMWeb / purple

Use for:

- broad employee audience;
- concise and task-focused content;
- guidance, tools and services;
- scannable and accessible information;
- content structured for CoMpanion / AI search.

### Digital Dashboard / green

Use for:

- role-specific information;
- branch or team resources;
- working material;
- collaborative content;
- document libraries;
- permissioned content.

### Rule

**Link, do not duplicate.**

A joined-up employee experience does not require everything to live in the same system.

## Benchmarking constraint

Benchmarking should inform ideas, not dictate implementation.

Before adopting a pattern, test:

1. what employee need it addresses;
2. whether it aligns with current CoMWeb governance direction;
3. whether an authoritative source already exists;
4. whether the pattern is feasible in SharePoint;
5. whether it supports accessibility;
6. who would own and maintain it;
7. whether it reduces or adds complexity.

Working rule:

**Benchmark the intent, not necessarily the implementation.**

## Prototype scenarios

Use realistic employee tasks, including:

- I need CX help but do not know which capability I need.
- I want to find a CX tool or resource.
- I need customer or community insight.
- I need support to improve a customer experience.
- I need to understand what happens next or who to contact.
- I need to reach an authoritative source or approved service pathway.

## AI-assisted navigation experiment

Status: Future-state experience hypothesis

### Purpose

Test whether conversational navigation could help employees find and use CX information more effectively while preserving the principle of one authoritative source.

This experiment is part of the CX employee experience design. It does not establish a requirement for a standalone CX AI agent or custom AI platform.

### Experience hypothesis

Employees often arrive with a need or task rather than knowing which CX capability they need, where the relevant information lives, what terminology to search for, which source is authoritative, or what they should do next.

A conversational navigation layer may provide another way into the same governed CX information architecture.

**Employee need → conversational guidance → authoritative source or service → clear next step**

This complements rather than replaces:

**Employee need → navigation / search → authoritative source or service → clear next step**

### Architectural principle

The conversational experience should not become a new source of organisational knowledge. It should interpret employee intent, retrieve from approved organisational content, provide concise orientation where appropriate, identify and link to the authoritative source or service, make uncertainty visible, and avoid unsupported organisational guidance.

The underlying principle remains:

> **One authoritative source, multiple ways in.**

Potential entry points may include navigation, search, CoMpanion, direct links and conversational assistance. Technology should not be selected until employee need, content readiness, governance requirements and organisational platform constraints have been tested.

### Prototype scenarios

Use representative employee tasks already aligned with the emerging experience model:

1. Finding the right CX capability — “I need CX help but don't know which team or capability I need.”
2. Finding a CX resource — “Where can I find the journey mapping toolkit?”
3. Finding customer or community insight — “I need customer insights for a project. Where should I start?”
4. Improving an experience — “I want to improve a customer experience. What support is available?”
5. Finding the next step or owner — “Who should I contact about this?” / “What do I need to do next?”
6. Finding current guidance — “What is the current CX guidance for this?”

Final test questions should be based on real employee needs and approved prototype content.

### Minimum viable experiment

Use a deliberately small content set rather than connecting the prototype to the entire branch information environment: approximately 10–20 representative content items with identifiable authoritative sources, spanning guidance, tools, resources and service pathways.

Compare, where practical:

**A — navigation / search:** employee attempts the task using the proposed information experience.

**B — conversational navigation:** employee expresses the same need conversationally and receives guidance to the authoritative source or service.

The purpose is not to prove conversation is universally better. It is to understand where each discovery mode helps employees.

### Evaluation criteria

Assess task success, correct authoritative source reached, time or steps to source, relevance, clarity of next step, employee confidence, unsupported or incorrect answers, situations requiring human assistance, and content or information-architecture problems exposed by the test.

### Content failure diagnostic

Where the conversational experience cannot provide a reliable answer, classify the reason rather than treating every failure as an AI failure.

Possible failure types include missing content, duplicate or competing sources, unclear authority or ownership, terminology mismatch, outdated or inaccessible content, poor content structure, ambiguous employee need, retrieval failure despite suitable content, or a request requiring human judgement or specialist support.

These findings should feed back into the content audit, information architecture and governance work.

### Design implication

The future CX information architecture should be tested for more than visual navigation. Where feasible, content should be understandable and retrievable through human navigation, enterprise search, CoMpanion or equivalent organisational AI search, direct links, and potential conversational interfaces.

This does not mean content should be rewritten primarily for AI. The objective is a clear, well-governed information environment that works across multiple discovery modes.

### Decision after the experiment

The experiment should inform whether existing navigation and search meet the employee need; content structure or governance should be improved first; existing organisational AI capabilities can provide useful conversational navigation; a specialised CX conversational experience warrants further investigation; or no additional AI capability provides enough value to justify its complexity and maintenance.

A permanent CX AI agent, custom development or new knowledge store should not be assumed before this evidence exists.
